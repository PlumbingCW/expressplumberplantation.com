How to Install Bathroom Sink Plumbing in Plantation, FL

Swapping a vanity or faucet is a manageable project when you take the steps in order and test every joint.

By the Plantation Express Plumbing team · Published 2026-10-12 · Updated 2026-10-12

A bathroom sink has two systems. The supply side brings hot and cold water to the faucet, and the drain side carries wastewater down through a trap to the wall. Most installs involve both.

This guide follows the order that avoids leaks. For bigger jobs, our plumbing installation team does the work and handles permits.

Gather parts before you start

  • The faucet and a pop-up drain assembly.
  • Two braided supply lines sized for the shutoff valves.
  • A P-trap kit and a tailpiece extension if needed.
  • Plumber's putty or the gaskets supplied with the drain.
  • A basin wrench, adjustable pliers and a bucket.

Shut off the water and remove the old parts

Close both shutoff valves under the sink. Open the faucet to drain the lines. Place the bucket under the trap and unscrew the slip nuts. Disconnect the supply lines at the valves, and use the basin wrench to loosen the faucet nuts. Lift out the old faucet and drain.

If the shutoff valves will not turn or leak when moved, stop and replace them. A fresh install on a bad valve is a flood waiting to happen.

Install the faucet and the drain on the sink

It is easier to do this before the sink goes onto the vanity. Set the faucet in the holes with its gasket, tighten the nuts from below and connect the supply lines. Then set the drain flange with a thin bead of plumber's putty, tighten the nut and trim the putty.

Attach the pop-up rod through the back of the drain body and connect it to the lever behind the faucet.

Connect the supply lines

  1. Hand tighten each braided line to the faucet inlet and then to the shutoff valve.
  2. Give each connection a gentle quarter turn with a wrench, no more.
  3. Turn on the water slowly and check each joint with a dry tissue.
  4. Run the faucet to clear debris, then remove the aerator and flush it once.

Build the trap and test the drain

The P-trap is a curved section that holds water and blocks sewer gas. Align the trap arm with the wall stub and the tailpiece, and tighten the slip nuts by hand with a small snug from pliers. Fill the sink, pull the plug and watch every joint as the water drains. A drip means a loose nut or a misaligned washer.

Choosing the faucet and drain parts

Match the faucet to the number of holes in the sink or counter. Single-hole, three-hole widespread and centerset designs are not interchangeable. Check the clearance behind the faucet for the handle and the pop-up rod. Choose a pop-up drain that fits the sink, since some vessel sinks need a specific type.

Brass fittings last longer than plastic in high use. Braided stainless supply lines are flexible and easy to fit.

Common trouble spots

  • A shutoff valve that does not close all the way.
  • A drain that sits too high or too low for the trap to align.
  • A faucet nut that cannot be reached without a basin wrench.
  • A pop-up rod that binds because it sits at the wrong angle.
  • A trap that is crooked and holds water.

Final checks

Run hot and cold water for a full minute. Look at the supply lines and the trap with a flashlight and a dry tissue. Check again after an hour and again the next morning, because slow leaks reveal themselves with time. Tighten any joint that shows moisture. A small drip now prevents rot in the cabinet later.

Wall-hung, pedestal and vanity sinks

A pedestal sink exposes the pipes, so a neat install matters. A wall-hung sink needs a solid backing in the wall, since the sink carries its own weight and often a leaning person. A vanity hides the pipes, but the cabinet back may need to be notched to clear the supply stubs and drain.

Check which type you have before you buy a faucet, and measure the distance from the wall to the center of the drain.

Water pressure and aerators

If pressure is low after the install, remove the aerator and clear any debris. New construction often leaves bits of solder, tape or sediment in the line. Run each supply for a minute with the aerator off, then replace it. If pressure is still low, check that the shutoff valve is fully open.

A simple order of work for the afternoon

  1. Measure, buy and lay out the parts on a towel.
  2. Shut off water and remove the old faucet and drain.
  3. Dry-fit the new drain and trap before you seal anything.
  4. Install the faucet and drain on the sink, then set the sink.
  5. Connect supply lines and the trap, then test twice.

Allow two to three hours for a first-time install. If a step goes wrong, stop, turn off the water and call. A half-finished sink is easier for us to fix than a flooded vanity.

Code notes for sink plumbing

Florida follows the Florida Building Code, Plumbing, which is based on the International Plumbing Code (IPC). That code sets the trap, vent and drain rules, so check them before you close the wall.

When to call a plumber

Call if the wall stub is corroded, if the drain does not line up with the new trap, if you find galvanized pipe or if a leak will not stop. Moving a sink to a new location calls for new supply and drain lines and sometimes new venting, and that is licensed work. We also repair existing fixtures through faucet and sink repair.
